ASSISTANT
The run started with a simple question: **if we know the coupling \(m\), can we calculate how pooling more neurons changes both variance and timescale?** For a specified network and clustering rule, yes. The two exponents come from the same modes, but they measure different properties of those modes. Here’s how the calculation works.

## 1. Turn the network into independent modes

I used the linear stochastic model from the browser chat,

\[
x_{t+1}=mBx_t+\eta_t,
\]

where \(x_t\) is the vector of neural activities, \(B\) is a symmetric connectivity matrix scaled to have spectral radius one, and \(\eta_t\) is independent, unit-variance white noise. The model is stationary for \(0\le m<1\).

Because \(B\) is symmetric, it has orthogonal eigenmodes \(u_i\) with eigenvalues \(\mu_i\). Projecting the activity onto mode \(i\) turns the network equation into a scalar equation:

\[
y_i(t+1)=a_i y_i(t)+\epsilon_i(t),
\qquad a_i=m\mu_i.
\]

Each mode retains a fraction \(a_i\) of its previous activity. Its stationary variance \(v_i\) therefore satisfies \(v_i=a_i^2v_i+1\), which gives

\[
v_i=\frac{1}{1-a_i^2}.
\]

Its normalized autocorrelation at lag \(\ell\) is \(a_i^\ell\). Summing from lag zero gives the integrated timescale I used in this run:

\[
\tau_i=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}a_i^\ell=\frac{1}{1-a_i}.
\]

This is where \(m\) enters both calculations. **Variance depends on \(a_i^2\), while the timescale depends on the sign of \(a_i\).** A mode near \(+1\) persists. A mode near \(-1\) can have equally large variance while flipping sign every step, so its autocorrelations cancel in the sum.

## 2. Calculate what a cluster of \(K\) neurons measures

A cluster sums the activity of its members. For a cluster \(C\), the coefficient of mode \(i\) in that sum is \(\sum_{j\in C}u_i(j)\). Square it and average across all clusters of size \(K\) to get the overlap \(q_i(K)\).

The contribution of mode \(i\) to the mean cluster variance is then

\[
s_i(K,m)=\frac{q_i(K)}{1-m^2\mu_i^2}.
\]

The calculation separates two effects. **The clustering determines \(q_i(K)\). The dynamics determine how much variance that overlap picks up at a given \(m\).** Adding the contributions gives the mean cluster variance \(V\). Dividing by \(V\) gives each mode’s share \(p_i\):

\[
V=\sum_i s_i,\qquad p_i=\frac{s_i}{V}.
\]

The integrated timescale of the variance-weighted cluster average is

\[
T=\sum_i p_i\tau_i.
\]

So larger clusters have longer timescales when they shift the variance weights \(p_i\) toward longer-lived modes. This is the quantity I fitted in the main sweep. Averaging each cluster’s normalized timescale equally is another possible estimator, and I computed that too.

## 3. Derive the link between \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\)

The local exponents are the slopes of \(\log V\) and \(\log T\) against \(\log K\). Define \(e_i=d\log q_i/d\log K\), the rate at which a cluster’s squared overlap with mode \(i\) grows. At fixed \(m\), the variance factor for each mode is fixed, so \(s_i\) grows at the same rate \(e_i\). Differentiating the sum gives

\[
\alpha_{\rm local}
=\frac{d\log V}{d\log K}
=\sum_i p_i e_i.
\]

For \(\beta\), the question is how the *shares* change. Since \(p_i=s_i/V\),

\[
\frac{dp_i}{d\log K}=p_i(e_i-\alpha_{\rm local}).
\]

A mode gains share if its overlap grows faster than the overall variance. Differentiate \(T=\sum_i p_i\tau_i\) using that result:

\[
\boxed{\displaystyle
\beta_{\rm local}
=\frac{\sum_i p_i(e_i-\alpha_{\rm local})\tau_i}{\sum_i p_i\tau_i}
=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}_p(e_i,\tau_i)}{\mathbb E_p[\tau_i]}.}
\]

**This is the main mathematical finding.** \(\alpha\) is the average growth rate of modal overlaps, weighted by variance. \(\beta\) is positive when the overlaps that grow fastest belong disproportionately to slow modes. If the same slow mode already dominates at every \(K\), doubling \(K\) can quadruple its variance and leave its timescale essentially flat. That gives \(\alpha\approx2\) and \(\beta\approx0\).

In the actual sweeps, \(K\) took discrete values such as \(2,4,8,16,32\). I fitted log–log slopes directly. A useful identity is \(W=VT=\sum_i s_i\tau_i\), where \(W\) is the summed autocovariance. On the same fitting window, \(\log T=\log W-\log V\), so the fitted \(\beta\) is exactly the slope of \(\log W\) minus the slope of \(\log V\).

## 4. Use the ring to connect the exponents directly to \(m\)

On the ring, we can identify which modes become slow and how far their correlations extend. The long-wavelength modes have decay rates approximately

\[
1-m\mu(k)\simeq \delta+Dk^2,
\qquad \delta=1-m.
\]

The two terms are comparable at \(k\sim\sqrt{\delta/D}\), which gives a correlation length \(\xi=\sqrt{D/\delta}\). This is the origin of the square-root law. It relies on the ring’s **quadratic** dependence on wavenumber \(k\).

In the continuum approximation, the covariance at separation \(r\) decays as \(e^{-r/\xi}\). For a contiguous interval of length \(K\), there are proportionally \(K-r\) pairs at separation \(r\), so

\[
V(K)\propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}
\int_0^K (K-r)e^{-r/\xi}\,dr
\propto \delta^{-3/2}\bigl(u-1+e^{-u}\bigr),
\qquad u=\frac{K}{\xi},
\]

where the last proportionality holds at fixed \(D\). Taking the log slope gives

\[
\alpha_{\rm local}(u)
=\frac{u(1-e^{-u})}{u-1+e^{-u}}.
\]

There’s a shortcut for the timescale. Each continuum mode contributes a factor \(1/(\delta+Dk^2)\) to variance and a second such factor to summed autocovariance. Differentiating the first factor with respect to \(\delta\) produces the second, so \(W=-\partial_\delta V\). Using \(T=W/V\), and noting that \(u\propto\sqrt{\delta}\) at fixed \(K\), gives

\[
\delta T=\frac{3-\alpha_{\rm local}}2,\qquad
\boxed{\displaystyle \beta_{\rm local}
=-\frac{d\alpha_{\rm local}/d\log u}{3-\alpha_{\rm local}}.}

explain how scale free networks are made - and what the main parameters are


ASSISTANT
A **scale-free network** has many nodes with few connections and a small number of highly connected **hubs**. The number of connections a node has is its **degree**, written \(k\).

The defining idea is that the degree distribution approximately follows a power law,

\[
P(k)\propto k^{-\gamma}.
\]

Here \(P(k)\) is the probability that a randomly selected node has degree \(k\), and \(\gamma\) controls how quickly highly connected nodes become rare. Smaller \(\gamma\) generally means a heavier tail and more extreme hubs.

A common way to build one is the **Barabási–Albert model**, which uses growth and preferential attachment. This is the method I used in the reconstructed scale-free networks in our run.

1. **Start with a small connected network.** I started with four nodes, all connected to one another. Each initially has degree 3.

2. **Add one new node.** Give it a fixed number of connections to existing nodes. Let’s call that number \(L\), keeping it distinct from our dynamical coupling \(m\).

3. **Prefer existing nodes with high degree.** The probability of choosing existing node \(j\) is proportional to its degree,
   \[
   p_j=\frac{k_j}{\sum_r k_r}.
   \]
   For example, if existing degrees are \(6,3,2,1\), their probabilities for the first connection are \(6/12,3/12,2/12,1/12\). The degree-6 node is six times as likely to receive that connection as the degree-1 node.

4. **Repeat until there are \(N\) nodes.** Nodes that gain connections become more attractive to subsequent nodes. This produces hubs.

The main parameters for this construction are

| Parameter | Meaning | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| \(N\) | Final number of nodes | Sets network size |
| \(L\) | Connections made by each new node | Sets density, with mean degree approaching \(2L\) |
| Initial network | Starting nodes and connections | Influences early hubs and finite-size structure |
| Random seed | Controls the random attachment choices | Produces different realizations with the same parameters |

The mean degree approaches \(2L\) because each new node adds \(L\) edges, and each edge contributes 1 to the degree of each endpoint.

**In our run, I used \(L=3\)**, so the mean degree was approximately 6. I grew networks to \(N=256\) and \(N=1024\), starting from the four-node fully connected network.

For the standard Barabási–Albert construction, the large-network degree distribution has approximately

\[
P(k)\propto k^{-3}.
\]

So **\(\gamma\approx3\) emerges from the attachment rule**. Changing \(L\) changes the density while retaining that asymptotic exponent. Other constructions let you choose \(\gamma\) directly.

To connect this to our dynamics, the construction first produces an adjacency matrix \(A\). I then normalized it,

\[
B=\frac{A}{\rho(A)},
\]

where \(\rho(A)\) is its largest absolute eigenvalue, and used

\[
x_{t+1}=mBx_t+\eta_t.
\]

Thus **\(L\) controls how the network is built**, while **\(m\) controls how strongly activity propagates through the completed network**.

Yes—that’s exactly how preferential attachment works.

At the start, equally connected nodes have equal chances of receiving a new connection. Random choices give some nodes a lead. Once a node has more connections, it becomes more likely to receive the next one, reinforcing that lead.

For example, if two nodes have degrees 4 and 2, the degree-4 node is twice as likely to be chosen for a new connection.

**You specify the attachment rule, and the degree distribution emerges as the network grows.** You don’t assign each node its final degree beforehand.

Older nodes also have an advantage because they’ve had more opportunities to gain connections, though chance affects which ones become the biggest hubs.

ASSISTANT
The ring derivation showed **how the same network can produce different \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) as you change either the coupling \(m\) or the sizes of clusters you measure.** We could calculate the mechanism explicitly because the ring has a simple spatial structure.

On the ring, correlations decay over a characteristic distance \(\xi\). Near instability,

\[
\xi\propto\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-m}}.
\]

So increasing \(m\) makes activity correlated across a larger portion of the ring. What matters for a cluster of \(K\) neighbouring nodes is the ratio

\[
u=\frac{K}{\xi}.
\]

The calculation gave three regimes.

| Cluster size | Variance scaling \(\alpha\) | Timescale scaling \(\beta\) |
|---|---|---|
| \(K\ll\xi\) | Near 2, because nodes fluctuate together | Near 0, because clusters already sample similar slow activity |
| \(K\sim\xi\) | Between 1 and 2 | Positive, because larger clusters increasingly emphasize slow modes |
| \(K\gg\xi\) | Near 1, because clusters combine many weakly correlated regions | Approaches 0 as the timescale saturates |

**The intermediate regime produces a peak in \(\beta\).** For local slopes, that peak occurred at approximately

\[
(\alpha,\beta)=(1.521,\;0.217),
\]

close to the empirical pair we’ve been discussing. Fitting across a finite range of cluster sizes changed the peak to about \((1.547,0.179)\) for a 16-fold window.

Its potential value for your project is that it provides a **worked mathematical explanation for why the two exponents can approach their empirical values together**. As the observation scale crosses the correlation scale, variance becomes less coherent while pooling continues to gain timescale. Both effects come from the same changing mixture of modes.

It also gives a testable prediction. Since

\[
u\propto K\sqrt{1-m},
\]

doubling the cluster sizes should require approximately

\[
1-m_{\mathrm{new}}\approx\frac{1-m_{\mathrm{old}}}{4}
\]

to recover the same part of the crossover. For example, a feature at \(m=0.99\) should move toward \(m=0.9975\). We can test whether your other architectures show an analogous shift.

The limitation is that this derivation assumes contiguous clusters and a diffusive ring spectrum. Your correlation-based clustering and hierarchical networks can weight modes differently. **The ring supplies an explicit mechanism and a benchmark for the other networks.** Establishing how broadly that mechanism explains your results still requires matching their spectra, clustering rules and timescale estimator.

2. Calculate what a cluster of \(K\) neurons measures
A cluster sums the activity of its members. For a cluster \(C\), the coefficient of mode \(i\) in that sum is \(\sum_{j\in C}u_i(j)\). Square it and average across all clusters of size \(K\) to get the overlap \(q_i(K)\).

\[
s_i(K,m)=\frac{q_i(K)}{1-m^2\mu_i^2}.
\]
The calculation separates two effects. The clustering determines \(q_i(K)\). The dynamics determine how much variance that overlap picks up at a given \(m\). Adding the contributions gives the mean cluster variance \(V\). Dividing by \(V\) gives each mode’s share \(p_i\):
\[
V=\sum_i s_i,\qquad p_i=\frac{s_i}{V}.
\]
The integrated timescale of the variance-weighted cluster average is
\[
T=\sum_i p_i\tau_i.
\]
So larger clusters have longer timescales when they shift the variance weights \(p_i\) toward longer-lived modes. This is the quantity I fitted in the main sweep. Averaging each cluster’s normalized timescale equally is another possible estimator, and I computed that too.


ASSISTANT
**The \(1\) is still the noise variance driving each mode. The new factor \(q_i(K)\) appears because we’re calculating how much of that mode is present in a cluster’s summed activity.**

Using our cleaner notation, mode \(i\) has amplitude \(z_i(t)\), pattern vector \(v_i\), and eigenvalue \(\lambda_i\). Its amplitude variance is

\[
\operatorname{Var}(z_i)=\frac{1}{1-m^2\lambda_i^2}.
\]

Now consider how it contributes to individual neurons. The contribution to neuron \(j\) is

\[
x_j^{(i)}(t)=v_i(j)\,z_i(t).
\]

Here \(v_i(j)\) is just a number specifying how strongly neuron \(j\) participates in that mode.

If we sum the neurons in cluster \(C\), the mode contributes

\[
\sum_{j\in C}x_j^{(i)}(t)
=
\underbrace{\left(\sum_{j\in C}v_i(j)\right)}_{\text{cluster's coefficient for this mode}}
z_i(t).
\]

Call that coefficient \(b_{Ci}\). The cluster picks up \(b_{Ci}z_i(t)\) from the mode. Multiplying a random quantity by \(b_{Ci}\) multiplies its variance by \(b_{Ci}^2\), giving

\[
\operatorname{Var}\bigl(b_{Ci}z_i\bigr)
=b_{Ci}^2\operatorname{Var}(z_i)
=\frac{b_{Ci}^2}{1-m^2\lambda_i^2}.
\]

For example, take the two-neuron pattern

\[
v_i=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}
\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Each neuron receives \(z_i/\sqrt2\) from this mode. Summing both neurons gives

\[
\frac{z_i}{\sqrt2}+\frac{z_i}{\sqrt2}
=\sqrt2\,z_i.
\]

Consequently, this mode contributes **twice its amplitude variance** to the cluster sum,

\[
\operatorname{Var}(\sqrt2\,z_i)
=\frac{2}{1-m^2\lambda_i^2}.
\]

For the opposite-sign pattern \(v_i=(1,-1)^\top/\sqrt2\), summing those two neurons gives zero. Its contribution to that cluster’s variance is zero, even though the mode itself fluctuates.

Finally, \(q_i(K)\) is the average of these squared coefficients across clusters of size \(K\),

\[
q_i(K)=\left\langle b_{Ci}^2\right\rangle_{|C|=K}.
\]

That gives the mean contribution

\[
\boxed{s_i(K,m)=q_i(K)\operatorname{Var}(z_i)
=\frac{q_i(K)}{1-m^2\lambda_i^2}.}
\]

**The denominator describes how much the mode fluctuates. The numerator describes how strongly the cluster sum picks up those fluctuations, including any cancellation between neurons.**

\frac{q_i(K)\operatorname{Var}(z_i)}

ASSISTANT
**\(K\) is the number of neurons in a cluster. To connect it to a mode’s weight, we need to know which neurons are in that cluster and their entries in the mode’s pattern.** Cluster size alone doesn’t determine the overlap.

Take one cluster \(C\) containing \(K\) neurons. For mode \(i\), its overlap coefficient is

\[
b_{Ci}=\sum_{j\in C}v_i(j).
\]

Squaring gives the variance multiplier,

\[
q_{Ci}=\left(\sum_{j\in C}v_i(j)\right)^2.
\]

Expanding that square shows exactly how the individual neurons contribute,

\[
\boxed{
q_{Ci}
=
\underbrace{\sum_{j\in C}v_i(j)^2}_{\text{individual contributions}}
+
\underbrace{2\sum_{\substack{j<k\\j,k\in C}}v_i(j)v_i(k)}_{\text{reinforcement or cancellation}}.
}
\]

So adding a neuron changes the overlap according to both its own weight and its sign relative to the neurons already in the cluster.

For example, suppose every neuron in the cluster has the same mode entry \(c\). Then

\[
b_{Ci}=Kc,
\qquad
\boxed{q_{Ci}=K^2c^2}.
\]

That is a direct relationship between \(K\) and the overlap. If the mode is spread equally across all \(N\) neurons, normalization gives \(c=1/\sqrt N\), so \(q_{Ci}=K^2/N\).

If the entries have different signs, the result changes. Two neurons with entries \(+0.1,+0.1\) give

\[
q_{Ci}=(0.1+0.1)^2=0.04,
\]

whereas two with entries \(+0.1,-0.1\) give

\[
q_{Ci}=(0.1-0.1)^2=0.
\]

Both clusters have \(K=2\), but they capture this mode very differently.

There is also an exact connection to \(K\) **across all modes**. Because the mode vectors form a complete orthonormal basis,

\[
\boxed{\sum_i q_{Ci}=K.}
\]

A cluster of \(K\) neurons therefore has a total squared overlap of \(K\), distributed across the modes. This means

\[
\frac{q_{Ci}}{K}
\]

is the fraction of that total overlap assigned to mode \(i\). It lies between 0 and 1. Averaging over clusters preserves the identity,

\[
\sum_i q_i(K)=K.
\]

To obtain \(q_i(K)\) for your network, we therefore construct the clusters at each size, sum each mode’s entries within each cluster, square those sums, and average. **A formula depending only on \(K\) becomes possible when we also specify the mode structure and the clustering rule**, as we did for contiguous clusters on the ring.

what is dales law


ASSISTANT
**Dale’s law says that a neuron generally has the same type of effect on its downstream targets—excitatory or inhibitory.**

- An **excitatory neuron** tends to increase the activity of the neurons it connects to.
- An **inhibitory neuron** tends to decrease their activity.

In a network model, this usually means **all outgoing weights from a given neuron have the same sign**.

Using our convention that \(W_{ij}\) is the influence of neuron \(j\) on neuron \(i\), each **column** of \(W\) must therefore contain either nonnegative or nonpositive weights. Connection strengths can vary, and some can be zero.

For our work, this matters because **Dale’s law and symmetric connectivity are different assumptions**. A connection between an excitatory neuron and an inhibitory neuron generally has opposite signs in the two directions. A network containing those connections therefore cannot have a symmetric weight matrix, so our orthogonal-mode derivation would need extending.

The biological rule has exceptions and qualifications involving neurotransmitter co-release and receptor effects. The same-sign outgoing-weight constraint is its standard simplified implementation in neural network models.

import pandas as pd
from scipy.linalg import eigh

sys.path.insert(0, HERE)
import linear_theory as lt
import spectral_classes as sc
import extended_networks as xn

OUT = os.path.join(HERE, "sweep_data", "matched_fitted.csv")

N = 512
SEED = 1
LMS = np.logspace(np.log10(1e-4), np.log10(0.2), 20)
_L = int(np.log2(N))


def hm_degree(phi):
    r = 2.0 / phi
    return _L if abs(r - 1) < 1e-12 else (r ** _L - 1) / (r - 1)


PHIS = np.linspace(1.0, 5.0, 60)
DEG_LO, DEG_HI = min(hm_degree(p) for p in PHIS), max(hm_degree(p) for p in PHIS)


def even_grid(count, lo, hi, cap):
    """Log-spaced even integers in [2, cap], deduplicated."""
    vals = np.logspace(np.log10(max(lo, 2)), np.log10(min(hi, cap)), count)
    out = sorted({int(min(max(2, 2 * round(v / 2)), cap)) for v in vals})
    return out


DEG10 = [float(v) for v in np.logspace(np.log10(DEG_LO), np.log10(DEG_HI), 10)]
K10 = even_grid(10, DEG_LO, DEG_HI, N - 2)
PS20 = np.logspace(-3, 0, 20)
GAMMAS20 = np.linspace(2.1, 3.5, 20)


def levels_for(A):
    ev, V = eigh(A, check_finite=False)
    if np.max(np.abs(ev)) == 0:
        return None
    return ev, V


def fits(ev, V, rows, **meta):
    for lm in LMS:
        S, L = lt.stationary_covariances(ev, V, 1.0 - lm)
        try:
            f = lt.fit_scaling(lt.coarse_grain(S, L, 4))
        except Exception:
            continue
        rows.append(dict(one_minus_m=lm, alpha_fit=f["alpha"],
                         beta_fit=f["beta"], **meta))


def main():
    rows = []
    print(f"matched degree range: {DEG_LO:.3f} to {DEG_HI:.1f}", flush=True)

    for k in even_grid(60, DEG_LO, DEG_HI, N - 2):
        A = lt.make_network(N, "ring", k, SEED)
        r = levels_for(A)
        if r:
            fits(*r, rows, family="ring", param=k, param2=np.nan,
                 degree=A.sum(1).mean())
    print(f"  ring done ({len(rows)} rows)", flush=True)

    for phi in PHIS:
        A = lt.make_network(N, "hm", float(phi), SEED)
        r = levels_for(A)
        if r:
            fits(*r, rows, family="hm", param=round(float(phi), 4),
                 param2=np.nan, degree=A.sum(1).mean())
    print(f"  hm done ({len(rows)} rows)", flush=True)

    for d in np.logspace(np.log10(DEG_LO), np.log10(DEG_HI), 60):
        A = lt.make_network(N, "er", float(min(d, N - 1.0)), SEED)
        r = levels_for(A)
        if r:
            fits(*r, rows, family="er", param=round(float(d), 4),
                 param2=np.nan, degree=A.sum(1).mean())
    print(f"  er done ({len(rows)} rows)", flush=True)

    for k in K10:
        for p in PS20:
            A = xn.small_world(N, k, float(p), SEED)
            r = levels_for(A)
            if r:
                fits(*r, rows, family="sw", param=k,
                     param2=round(float(p), 6), degree=A.sum(1).mean())
        print(f"    sw k={k} done ({len(rows)} rows)", flush=True)
    print(f"  sw done ({len(rows)} rows)", flush=True)

    for d in DEG10:
        for g in GAMMAS20:
            A = xn.scale_free(N, float(d), float(g), SEED)
            r = levels_for(A)
            if r:
                fits(*r, rows, family="sf", param=round(float(d), 4),
                     param2=round(float(g), 4), degree=A.sum(1).mean())
        print(f"    sf degree={d:.2f} done ({len(rows)} rows)", flush=True)

    df = pd.DataFrame(rows)
    df = df[np.isfinite(df.alpha_fit) & np.isfinite(df.beta_fit)].copy()
    df["dist"] = sc.distance(df.alpha_fit.values, df.beta_fit.values)
    os.makedirs(os.path.dirname(OUT), exist_ok=True)
    df.to_csv(OUT, index=False)
    print(f"\nwrote {len(df)} rows to {OUT}")
    print(df.groupby("family").agg(best=("dist", "min"),
                                   deg_lo=("degree", "min"),
                                   deg_hi=("degree", "max")))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

"""Small-world and scale-free generators with the density freed.

linear_theory.make_network hardcodes small-world degree to 4 and scale-free
mean degree to 6, so neither can be swept over density. These two functions
reproduce those recipes EXACTLY, with the hardcoded constant replaced by an
argument. Nothing else differs: the same ring-lattice start, the same rewiring
rule and draw order for sw; the same Chung-Lu rank weights and the same
lt.sample_edges for sf.

Only the network construction is reimplemented here. Everything downstream --
covariances, coarse-graining, fitting -- still goes through linear_theory
unchanged.
"""
import os
import sys

import numpy as np

HERE = os.path.dirname(os.path.abspath(__file__))
sys.path.insert(0, HERE)
import linear_theory as lt


def small_world(n, k, p, seed):
    """Watts-Strogatz: ring lattice of degree k, each local edge rewired w.p. p.

    Identical to linear_theory.make_network(..., 'sw', p, seed) when k = 4.
    """
    if k < 2 or k >= n or k % 2:
        raise ValueError("Small-world degree must be an even integer in [2, n).")
    if not 0 <= p <= 1:
        raise ValueError("Rewiring probability must be in [0, 1].")
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    adjacency = np.zeros((n, n))
    for offset in range(1, k // 2 + 1):
        for i in range(n):
            j = (i + offset) % n
            adjacency[i, j] = adjacency[j, i] = 1
    if p:
        for offset in range(1, k // 2 + 1):
            for i in range(n):
                j = (i + offset) % n
                if rng.random() < p:
                    candidates = np.flatnonzero(
                        (adjacency[i] == 0) & (np.arange(n) != i))
                    if candidates.size:
                        other = rng.choice(candidates)
                        adjacency[i, j] = adjacency[j, i] = 0
                        adjacency[i, other] = adjacency[other, i] = 1
    return adjacency


def scale_free(n, degree, gamma, seed):
    """Chung-Lu with rank weights r^(-1/(gamma-1)), rescaled to `degree`.

    Identical to linear_theory.make_network(..., 'sf', gamma, seed) when
    degree = 6. Note the min(.,1) clip: at high target degree or low gamma the
    realised mean degree falls below the target, so it is recorded separately.
    """
    if gamma <= 1:
        raise ValueError("Scale-free gamma must exceed 1.")
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    weights = np.arange(1, n + 1, dtype=float) ** (-1 / (gamma - 1))
    weights *= degree / weights.mean()
    probability = np.minimum(np.outer(weights, weights) / weights.sum(), 1)
    return lt.sample_edges(probability, rng)


def _check():
    """The k=4 / degree=6 cases must reproduce linear_theory exactly."""
    n = 256
    for p in (0.0, 0.05, 0.3, 1.0):
        a = small_world(n, 4, p, 7)
        b = lt.make_network(n, "sw", p, 7)
        print(f"  sw p={p:<6g} identical to make_network: {np.array_equal(a, b)}")
    for g in (2.1, 2.5, 3.5):
        a = scale_free(n, 6, g, 7)
        b = lt.make_network(n, "sf", g, 7)
        print(f"  sf gamma={g:<6g} identical to make_network: {np.array_equal(a, b)}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    _check()

**2. The interpretation of \(\beta\) is substantially sound**

The exact interval expression is

\[
\beta=
\log_2
\frac{\sum_i p_iR_i\tau_i}

\]

It measures whether pooling preferentially weights longer-lived activity. Equal mode timescales give \(\beta=0\), whatever \(\alpha\).

> Positive \(\beta\) requires pooling to shift the variance mixture toward slower modes.

Having several modes with different timescales is insufficient unless their overlap growth also favours the slower ones.

ASSISTANT
**We have an equation defining the peak for a specified model, but no general guarantee that it occurs at \(\alpha=1.5\).** Our results place it near that value in several regimes, with clear exceptions.

The local modal formulas show why. At fixed \(m\),

\[
\alpha=\mathbb E_p[e_i],
\qquad
\beta=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}_p(e_i,\tau_i)}
{\mathbb E_p[\tau_i]},
\]

where \(e_i\) measures how a mode’s overlap grows with cluster size. **Knowing the mean \(\alpha\) does not determine that covariance**, or where it reaches a maximum.

For the **continuum ring**, however, both exponents are explicit functions of \(u=K/\xi\),

\[
\alpha=A(u),\qquad
\beta=B(u)=-\frac{uA'(u)}{3-A(u)}.
\]

An interior peak must satisfy \(B'(u)=0\). Differentiating gives

\[
\boxed{
\bigl[A'(u)+uA''(u)\bigr]\,[3-A(u)]
+u[A'(u)]^2=0.
}
\]

Solving that condition numerically gives

\[
u_*\approx2.01405,\qquad
\alpha(u_*)\approx1.52095,\qquad
\beta(u_*)\approx0.21676.
\]

So the ring provides a **model-specific mathematical prediction** near \(1.5\). It does not give an exact identity at \(1.5\).

The distinction also matters for the hierarchy and global fits. Their peak conditions come from their own modal sums and fitting windows. Our checked 512-node examples include peak locations ranging from \(\alpha\approx1.33\) for a small ring interval to \(\alpha\approx1.53\) for some global hierarchy windows.

**The open theoretical question is why an intermediate range of these models repeatedly puts the peak near \(3/2\).** We have verified examples and exact formulas to investigate that regularity. We have yet to derive assumptions under which the peak is forced—or tightly bounded—to occur there.

ASSISTANT
**In the continuum calculation, \(d\) describes the dimension of an idealized spatial lattice.** It determines how neighbours, distances and clusters are organised.

- A **1D ring** has one coordinate. A contiguous cluster is an interval.
- A **2D lattice** has two coordinates. We used square clusters.
- A **3D lattice** has three coordinates. The corresponding clusters are cubes.

If a cluster has side length \(\ell\), its number of neurons scales as

\[
K\propto\ell^d.
\]

That is one reason \(d\) changes the exponent predictions.

**Going above 3 is mathematically straightforward.** A node in a 4D lattice has four integer coordinates,

\[
(i_1,i_2,i_3,i_4).
\]

For a nearest-neighbour lattice, it connects to nodes differing by \(+1\) or \(-1\) in one coordinate—eight neighbours altogether. We can simulate that graph without embedding it in ordinary physical space.

When discussing real neural networks, however, we might instead mean **spectral dimension**. This describes how many modes have eigenvalues close to the leading eigenvalue. In a suitable scaling regime,

\[
\frac{N_{\rm modes}(\varepsilon)}{N}
\propto\varepsilon^{d_s/2},
\]

where \(\varepsilon\) measures the eigenvalue gap from the top of the spectrum.

For ordinary diffusive lattices, spectral and spatial dimension agree. **For more general networks, spectral dimension can be non-integer or exceed 3**, even though the neurons occupy three-dimensional space. Long-range connections change the spectrum without requiring extra physical directions.

The qualification for our work is that **the continuum exponent formula uses more than spectral dimension**. It also assumes lattice-like modes and spatial box clusters. Substituting a measured \(d_s\) from an HM network into that formula is a hypothesis to test; matching \(d_s\) alone does not establish that the exponent predictions transfer.

explain the mathematical representation of rings and how you can extend it to non-integer


ASSISTANT
**We represent the ring as a connectivity matrix. The non-integer extension happens later, in the continuum formula derived from lattice models.** There is a separate mathematical step between those two things.

For a ring of \(N\) neurons, label the nodes

\[
i=0,1,\ldots,N-1.
\]

Indices wrap around: node \(N\) is node 0. In the simplest ring, neuron \(i\) connects to \(i-1\) and \(i+1\). Its adjacency matrix is

\[
A_{ij}=
\begin{cases}
1,&j=i\pm1\pmod N,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]

For our degree-four ring, we also include \(i\pm2\).

**The ring’s symmetry lets us write down its modes.** They are waves around the circle,

\[
v_q(j)=\frac1{\sqrt N}e^{iqj},
\qquad q=\frac{2\pi r}{N},
\quad r=0,\ldots,N-1.
\]

The complex notation packages sine and cosine waves together. For our normalized degree-four ring, the eigenvalue of each wave is

\[
\lambda(q)=\frac{\cos q+\cos 2q}{2}.
\]

For long-wavelength modes, \(q\) is small. Expanding the cosines gives

\[
\lambda(q)\approx1-\frac54q^2.
\]

This quadratic dependence produces the diffusion approximation we used.

**To extend the construction to integer dimension \(d\), we replace one circular coordinate with \(d\) circular coordinates.** A node has position

\[
\mathbf j=(j_1,\ldots,j_d),
\]

and a mode has wavevector \(\mathbf q=(q_1,\ldots,q_d)\). Near the top of the spectrum, its relaxation rate takes the form

\[
h(\mathbf q)=\delta+D|\mathbf q|^2,
\qquad
|\mathbf q|^2=q_1^2+\cdots+q_d^2,
\qquad \delta=1-m.
\]

A cluster is now a box with side length \(\ell\), containing approximately \(K=\ell^d\) nodes. In Fourier space, its squared overlap separates into one factor per coordinate,

\[
|H_\ell(\mathbf q)|^2
=
\prod_{a=1}^{d}
\frac{4\sin^2(q_a\ell/2)}{q_a^2}.
\]

We sum the modal variance contributions by integrating over wavevectors,

\[
V(\ell)\propto
\int_{\mathbb R^d}
\frac{|H_\ell(\mathbf q)|^2}
{\delta+D|\mathbf q|^2}\,d^dq.
\]

**The fractional extension becomes possible because this integral can be rewritten as a one-dimensional integral containing \(d\) as an ordinary exponent.**

Use the identity

\[
\frac1{\delta+D|\mathbf q|^2}
=
\int_0^\infty e^{-s\delta}e^{-sD|\mathbf q|^2}\,ds.
\]

Since the box overlap and exponential both separate by coordinate, the wavevector integral becomes a product of \(d\) identical integrals. After changing variables, the result has the form

\[
\boxed{
V(\ell)\propto
\ell^{d+2}
\int_0^\infty e^{-wx^2}g(w)^d\,dw,
\qquad x=\ell\sqrt{\delta/D},
}
\]

where

\[
g(w)=
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\frac{4\sin^2(u/2)}{u^2}e^{-wu^2}\,du.
\]

For integer \(d\), \(g(w)^d\) arose from multiplying together one contribution per coordinate direction.

For non-integer \(d\), we **define the extension** using

\[
g(w)^d=\exp\!\bigl(d\log g(w)\bigr).
\]

Because \(g(w)>0\), this is well-defined for values such as \(d=1.5\). We can evaluate the integral and derive exponent curves from it.

**That gives a continuous mathematical family of predictions.** At integer dimensions it corresponds to the continuum box calculation. At fractional dimensions it is a formal extension; it does not automatically correspond to a particular network. To connect it to a fractal or brain network, we would need to establish that the network’s modes and cluster overlaps reproduce the assumptions behind that formula.

can the ring sortof notion allow it to have a sortof sense of global projections - as it is recurrent in some sense but also i guess globally coupled


ASSISTANT
**Yes—a locally connected ring can support activity patterns spanning the whole network, because repeated local interactions propagate activity around it.** That gives it collective dynamics even though each neuron directly contacts only nearby neurons.

It helps to distinguish three things.

**Recurrence.** In our symmetric ring, a neuron influences neighbours that can influence it back. Activity can also travel around longer closed paths. Recurrence therefore exists even before a signal travels around the entire ring.

**Indirect global influence.** After one step, an input reaches immediate neighbours. After several steps, it can reach increasingly distant neurons. Mathematically, the effect of an initial perturbation after \(t\) steps is

\[
x_t=m^tB^t x_0.
\]

The matrix \(B^t\) describes propagation along paths of length \(t\). With enough steps, those paths connect distant parts of the ring. Whether an appreciable signal survives that journey depends on \(m\) and the network size.

**A global activity mode.** The regular ring has the uniform Perron mode,

\[
v_{\mathrm P}=\frac1{\sqrt N}(1,1,\ldots,1)^\top.
\]

This pattern involves every neuron moving together. Local recurrent connectivity supports it: when all neurons have the same activity, every neuron receives the same input from its neighbours. Its normalized eigenvalue is 1, so its amplitude evolves with multiplier \(m\).

**That global mode is a collective pattern, rather than a direct anatomical projection to every neuron.** The ring has network-wide modes, but its connections remain local.

If you want to represent actual diffuse or long-range projections, we can add them explicitly. For example,

\[
A=A_{\rm local}+g\,\frac{\mathbf1\mathbf1^\top}{N}
\]

adds uniform coupling between all neurons, with strength controlled by \(g\). Sparse shortcuts or projections from a separate population provide other constructions.

Those additions change the spectrum and can separate the uniform mode from the remaining modes. We would then recalculate the exponent curves.

For our question, this distinction matters: **a strong global mode can make activity highly collective, while positive \(\beta\) requires larger clusters to keep shifting their weight toward slower activity.** If the global mode already dominates every cluster size, there is little further timescale growth to gain from pooling.

For small \(q\),

\[

\]

| Choice | One option | Another option |
|---|---|---|
| Spatial description | Separate neurons on a lattice | Smooth continuum field |
| Network extent | Finite ring or periodic box | Infinite line or space |
| Time evolution | Discrete updates | Continuous time |
